# Facts: The merchant ship Ann Francis, sailing home from Spain, was caught in a storm on the evening of 28 December 1583, blown off course and wrecked on Margam beach near Port Talbot, south Wales. Her cargo included Spanish silver coins and gold coins of Ferdinand and Isabella. Centuries later, storms and dredging stripped the sand away and metal detectorist Peter Hughes recovered the hoard between 1996 and 2017; he found a brass combination lock there in 1999 and spent about two decades trying to solve it. The lock has four rotating rings of six letters each (1,296 combinations, one of which opens it). Amgueddfa Cymru - Museum Wales acquired the hoard and asked Cardiff University for help: David Williams (biomechanics research facility, School of Engineering) micro-CT scanned it without damage; the second scan failed, the third showed how the notches lined up and gave the code ANCE. Back at the museum the team set the rings, conservators cleared grit, and it opened for the first time in over four hundred years (announced 4 November 2026). Hughes told BBC Wales it was "like opening the Ark of the Covenant". Curator Alastair Willis: "we are left wondering if the code meant something to its owner". The lock is on show in "Tides of Treasure" at the National Waterfront Museum, Swansea. Reported by Smithsonian Magazine (12 November 2026) and The Jerusalem Post (7 November 2026).

This month, a brass padlock that stayed locked for over four hundred years clicked open, and nobody broke it.
In December fifteen eighty-three, the merchant ship Ann Francis was sailing home from Spain when a winter storm drove her onto a beach in Wales, and her silver and gold coins sank into the sand.
Long after, storms stripped the sand away, and a man with a metal detector began to find treasure.
In nineteen ninety-nine, he dug up a little brass lock with four turning rings of letters.
Six letters on each ring gives you more than a thousand guesses, and only one is right.
For about twenty years, nobody got it.
So the museum in Wales took it to a lab in Cardiff, where engineers put it in a scanner that sees through metal.
The second scan failed, but the third showed just where every notch lined up.
The code was A, N, C, E.
At the museum, they set the rings, cleaned out the grit, and it opened.
Nobody forced it, so the lock is still perfect, and it's on show in Swansea.
The man who found it said it was like opening the Ark of the Covenant.
But nobody knows what those four letters meant to its owner.
