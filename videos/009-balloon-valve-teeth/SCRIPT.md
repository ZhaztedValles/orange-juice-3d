# Facts: 5 September 1862. The meteorologist James Glaisher and the aeronaut Henry Tracy Coxwell ascended in Coxwell's coal-gas balloon (later called the Mammoth) from Wolverhampton, where there was a large coal-gas plant. They were 5 miles up in less than an hour. Glaisher made regular observations up to about 29,000 ft (his last barometer reading, 9 3/4 inches); air temperatures ranged from -40 C to -57 C. As the balloon kept ascending he lost his sight and then consciousness from oxygen deprivation. Coxwell had found the valve line twisted and climbed into the ring to free it; back in the car he had lost the use of his hands (frozen), so he seized the valve cord in his teeth and with several jerks of his head opened the valve to begin the descent. Glaisher regained consciousness and resumed his observations. The peak altitude is an estimate (Glaisher first said 37,000 ft, later 32,000 ft as more conservative; others give 31,000-36,000 ft), so the narration says only five miles. The ascent is credited with the altitude record of its time.
Sources: https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/research/library-and-archive/archive-hidden-treasures/james-glaisher, https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/henry-coxwell

<!-- narration -->
In eighteen sixty-two, a man saved two lives by pulling a rope with his teeth, five miles above England.
James Glaisher was a weather scientist, proud of his careful notes, and Henry Coxwell flew the giant balloon.
On the fifth of September they rose from the Wolverhampton gasworks, with a notebook and a basket of thermometers.
In under an hour they were five miles up, and the thermometer fell far below freezing.
Still the balloon climbed, and Glaisher wrote down every number.
Then his pencil slowed, his arms went limp, and he could no longer read the dials.
Coxwell saw the valve rope was tangled, and climbed up into the ring to free it.
When he came back down, he could not use his hands at all.
You see, the higher you go, the less air is piled above you, pressing down.
It is like a stack of pillows, squashed flat at the bottom and fluffy at the top.
Up there, every breath held so little air that your brain starts to switch off.
So Coxwell grabbed the valve rope in his teeth and jerked his head until the gas hissed out.
The balloon sank into thicker air, and Glaisher woke, picked up his pencil and took the next reading.
The scientist had slept right through the highest moment of his own record.
