# Facts: London docklands (Wellclose Square), 1829; Dr Nathaniel Ward sealed a sphinx moth chrysalis in damp soil in a glass bottle; the moth never emerged but grass and a fern sprouted; the plants lived about three years without water (grass bloomed and the fern had five fronds in year two) until the lid rusted; his garden plants had died in London's polluted air; in 1833 a sealed case of ferns went to Sydney and the captain reported the experiment had fully succeeded; Linda Hall Library gives typical survival of plants on long voyages as about 5 percent without the cases and about 95 percent with them.
Sources: https://arboretum.harvard.edu/stories/the-wardian-case-how-a-simple-box-moved-the-plant-kingdom/, https://www.linnean.org/news/2023/07/03/inside-wellclose-square-the-home-of-nathanial-b-ward

A London doctor grew a fern in a sealed glass bottle, and it lived for three years with no water at all.
It's eighteen twenty-nine, and Doctor Ward collects bugs in the smoky docks of London.
He buries a moth pupa in damp soil, drops it in a big glass bottle, and screws the lid on tight.
The moth never hatches, but a green speck pokes out of the soil.
Soon a fern unrolls, and a tuft of grass sprouts beside it.
But every plant Ward tries in his garden dies in the sooty air.
Yet nobody waters the bottle, and the fern keeps growing.
Ward is stumped, since nothing goes in, nothing comes out, and the garden won't die.
You see, a sealed bottle works like a bathroom mirror after a hot shower.
The water rises as steam, hits the cool glass, and runs back down, so it goes round and round.
In eighteen thirty-three Ward ships sealed cases of ferns to Sydney, and the captain writes that the test was a full success.
Before the cases, only about one plant in twenty lived through a long sea trip, and after, nearly all did.
Not bad for a moth that never showed up.
