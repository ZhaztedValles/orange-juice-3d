# Facts: On Saturday 31 December 1853 (New Year's Eve; the invitation said five o'clock) the sculptor Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins hosted a dinner "in the mould of the Iguanodon" in his model room at the Crystal Palace site at Sydenham, south London, where he was building life-size models of extinct animals for the Crystal Palace Company (the park opened in June 1854). Hawkins drew the invitation cards himself. Most reports say twenty-one men dined (some reports put part of the company at a side table; the Illustrated London News engraving of 7 January 1854 shows a platform round the model). Richard Owen sat in the head; Francis Fuller of the Crystal Palace Company at the hind end. Eight courses were listed (soups; fish; removes such as roast turkey, ham and pigeon pie; entrees; game; sweets; dessert; wines). Toasts to the Queen and Prince Albert; Owen proposed a toast to the memory of Gideon Mantell, answered in silence; a song by Edward Forbes with a chorus about "the jolly old beast", sung very loudly; Punch and the papers covered it. The Iguanodon was known from a few bones: Mary Ann and Gideon Mantell's teeth found in Sussex in 1822; a pointed bone was taken for a horn on the nose, and the Crystal Palace models, advised by Owen, followed that. In 1878 miners in a coal mine at Bernissart, Belgium, found about thirty Iguanodon skeletons over about three years, showing the spike was a thumb. The Crystal Palace sculptures still stand in the park (Grade I listed).

On New Year's Eve, eighteen fifty-three, twenty-one men sat down to dinner inside a dinosaur.
Their host was the sculptor Benjamin Waterhouse Hawkins, who was making huge model monsters for the new Crystal Palace park in London.
His pride was a giant Iguanodon, still a hollow clay mould in his workshop, so he set a table in its open back and drew every invite by hand.
The top scientist Richard Owen got the best seat, right in the head.
Then waiters passed up soup, fish, turkey and pie, eight courses in all.
They toasted the Queen and roared out a song about the jolly old beast, the papers loved it, and crowds came to see the monsters.
There was just one problem, the dinosaur was wrong.
You see, to build a beast from a few bones is like doing a jigsaw with no picture on the box.
One pointed bone had been found, and the experts guessed it was a horn, so the model got a spike on its nose.
Then in eighteen seventy-eight, miners in Belgium dug up about thirty skeletons, and the spike was on the hand, it was a thumb.
So the most famous dinner in science was eaten inside a mistake, and that mistake still stands in the park today, horn and all.
