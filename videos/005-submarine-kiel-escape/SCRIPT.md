# Facts: Kiel harbour, 1 February 1851. Wilhelm Bauer, formerly a corporal in the Bavarian artillery, designed the all-iron submarine Brandtaucher to act against the Danish blockade of Kiel; about 8 m long. Its propeller was driven by crewmen turning large treadwheels, and the trim was set by hauling a heavy ballast weight along the bilges; four small windows. With Bauer were his two assistants, Witt and Thomsen. At 9 a.m., about 14 minutes after setting out and shortly after flooding the flotation compartments, the boat lost its trim; the stern dropped, the ballast slid aft, the pressure crushed the starboard side and broke a propulsion drive wheel, and the boat came to rest stern-first at about 16 m, leaking through several seams. The two crewmen tried to plug the leaks and pump; Bauer realised that once the trapped air was compressed to the outside pressure the hatch could be opened, and persuaded them to stop and rest. Some four or more hours later, in the cold, near-dark air pocket in the bow, they heard chains and grappling hooks on the hull; prying the hatch with an iron bar let in a stream of cold water; the strongest of the three put his back to the hatch, it flew open, and the escaping air swept him out; Bauer grabbed the other by the hair and both were swept out; all three surfaced and were picked up by boats. It was the first submarine escape to be witnessed and reported. The wreck was raised in 1887 (some sources 1882) and is the oldest surviving submarine in the world.

In eighteen fifty-one three men sank to the bottom of Kiel harbour, and the only way out was to let the sea in.
Wilhelm Bauer had built an iron submarine to slip under the Danish ships blockading the port.
His two helpers, Witt and Thomsen, turned big wheels that drove the propeller.
One cold February morning they flooded the tanks and dived.
The stern tipped down, the heavy ballast weight slid back, and the boat dropped fast.
Then the sea crushed in one side and snapped a drive wheel.
They hit bottom about sixteen metres down, with water spraying through the seams.
Witt and Thomsen pumped and plugged, but the hatch would not budge.
Bauer told them to stop and let the water in.
You see, the sea was squeezing that hatch shut, like a freezer door that will not open right after you close it.
The rising water squeezed their trapped air tighter, until it pushed back as hard as the sea.
For four hours they waited in the dark, while grappling hooks scraped the hull.
Then the strongest man put his back to the hatch, and it flew open.
The air burst out and swept all three up to the surface, where boats pulled them in.
The boat stayed down for over thirty years, and today it is the oldest submarine in the world.
