# Facts: Siege of Paris by the Prussian army, 19 September 1870 to the armistice of 28 January 1871. Pierre-Charles Delort (patent filed 21 November 1870), Émile Robert and Jacques Vonoven devised the "boules de Moulins": hollow, watertight zinc balls about 12-13 cm across with vanes (fins) that made them spin in the current; each held about 500 letters (500-600 per Wikipedia). They were made to roll along the riverbed rather than float, so the Prussians watching the surface would not see them. Letters were collected at Moulins (Allier) and needed a 1-franc stamp instead of the usual 20 centimes. Robert, disguised as a farmer or poultry seller, dropped the balls into the Seine upstream, between Bray-sur-Seine and Samois; about fifty (55 per Wikipedia) went in between about 4-5 and 28-31 January 1871. A 280 m net at Port à l'Anglais (Alfortville), run by Vonoven, was to catch them. Ice slowed the current and on 22 January tore the net's anchors loose; it was repaired on 28 January, too late; the balls silted up, snagged or slipped through. None reached Paris during the siege. Between 25 and 30 have been found, the first at Les Andelys in March 1871, others in 1942, 1952, 1968, at Vatteville-la-Rue in 1982 and the last in 1988; letters were delivered to addressees or their descendants; around twenty are thought to remain in the river.

In the winter of eighteen seventy-one, the letters for Paris rolled along the bottom of a river.
Prussian soldiers surrounded the city, and no post could get in.
So three Frenchmen, Delort, Robert and Vonoven, had a strange plan.
In Moulins, people paid a whole franc to send one letter to Paris.
Hundreds of letters were sealed in a zinc ball with fins to make it spin.
Robert, dressed as a farmer, slipped them into the Seine, far upstream.
They sank and rolled along the riverbed, so the soldiers saw nothing.
In Paris, Vonoven stretched a net across the river and waited.
Day after day, the net came up empty.
Then one night, it was torn from its posts.
You see, that January was bitterly cold, and the river filled with ice.
A net can stop a ball, but ice pushes like a whole crowd leaning on a parade rope.
The posts gave way, and the slow current left the balls stuck in the mud.
The siege ended before a single ball arrived.
But they kept turning up for over a century, the last in nineteen eighty-eight.
Some letters finally reached the grandchildren of the people they were written to.
About twenty balls are still down there in the mud.
The slowest post in history is still on its way.
