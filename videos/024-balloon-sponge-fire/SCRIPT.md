# Facts: Paris, 21 November 1783. Jean-Francois Pilatre de Rozier and Francois Laurent, Marquis d'Arlandes, made the first free (untethered) flight by people, in a hot-air balloon built by the Montgolfier brothers (paper and cloth, decorated with gold royal emblems on blue). They took off from the garden of the Chateau de la Muette at the edge of the Bois de Boulogne, watched by the King and by Benjamin Franklin. Each man stood in the circular gallery on opposite sides of the balloon; they fed a straw fire in a brazier hung under the opening. d'Arlandes forgot to stoke the fire while looking at the view and the balloon sank; Pilatre told him that if he kept staring at the river he would soon be bathing in it (Guinness). Embers burned small glowing spots and holes in the skin; d'Arlandes put them out with a wet sponge on the tine of a pitchfork (Linda Hall; Guinness: both men used wet sponges); whenever he paused stoking, the balloon sank. They rose to about three thousand feet (Linda Hall, Wikipedia), crossed the Seine, and after it d'Arlandes let the fire die as the glowing spots kept spreading. After about twenty-five minutes they landed in a field between two windmills on the Butte-aux-Cailles, unhurt. The pilots drank champagne, a tradition balloonists still keep (Wikipedia). That evening d'Arlandes visited Franklin to tell him about the flight (Science History Institute). Distance differs between sources (about 9 km per Wikipedia and Linda Hall, 24 km per Guinness): not stated in the video.

The first two people ever to fly spent their whole flight putting out fires on their own balloon.
Paris, November, seventeen eighty-three.
In a royal garden, Pilatre de Rozier and the Marquis d'Arlandes step up to a huge paper balloon, blue and gold.
Under it hangs a fire of burning straw, the king is watching, and so is Benjamin Franklin.
Twenty-five minutes left.
The ropes let go, and up they go over the trees.
Twenty minutes left.
The marquis is so busy looking at Paris that he forgets the fire, and they sink.
Pilatre shouts that if he keeps looking at the river, he will soon be bathing in it.
Fifteen minutes left.
Up at three thousand feet, sparks from the straw burn small holes in the paper.
Ten minutes left.
The marquis sticks a wet sponge on a pitchfork and dabs out the fires, one by one.
But each time he stops feeding the fire, they drop toward the roofs.
Five minutes left.
They cross the Seine, and the burning spots keep spreading.
So he lets the fire die.
Zero.
They bump down in a field between two windmills, burnt but alive.
And the first thing they did after the first flight of all time was pop the champagne.
