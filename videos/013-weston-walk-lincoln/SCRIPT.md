# Facts: During the 1860 US presidential campaign, Edward Payson Weston (1839-1929), a young man from Providence living in Boston, bet his friend George B. Eddy on the election: if Lincoln won, Weston would walk from the Massachusetts State House in Boston to Washington, D.C. "inside of ten consecutive days" to see the inauguration. Lincoln won. Weston set out on 22 February 1861; several hundred people followed him down Beacon Street. The planned route was about 478 miles, about 50 miles a day, through snow, ice, rain and mud; he fell several times and sprained an ankle; crowds gathered in the towns, often hours early. He reached Washington at about 5 pm on 4 March 1861, a few hours after Lincoln was sworn in at the Capitol, missing his ten-day limit (10 days, 4 hours, 12 minutes by his own count; other accounts say about 10 days 10 hours). That evening he attended the inaugural ball; he later met Lincoln, who shook his hand and offered to pay his train fare home to Boston, which Weston declined. The walk made him famous, and for about five decades he was a professional walker, "the Pedestrian", the father of modern pedestrianism. The "nearly a million steps" figure is our own estimate from the distance.

A man lost a bet on an election, so he had to walk almost five hundred miles.
It's eighteen sixty, and Edward Payson Weston from Boston is sure Abraham Lincoln will never win.
He tells a friend that if Lincoln wins he will walk to Washington to see him sworn in, inside ten days.
Lincoln wins.
One cold February morning, he sets off from the State House, and hundreds follow him down the street.
He walks about fifty miles a day through snow and ice.
Whole towns wait for hours to watch one man go by.
He falls in the mud, sprains an ankle, and keeps going, but somehow he keeps falling behind.
You see, mud steals a little piece of every step.
Ever walked on soft sand at the beach? Your foot sinks and slides back, so each stride carries you less far and tires you more.
Over nearly a million steps, the hours vanish.
On the fourth of March, he limps into Washington at five in the evening, hours after Lincoln was sworn in.
He still made the inaugural ball, and the new president shook his hand.
Lincoln even offered to buy him a train ticket home, but Weston said no.
The man who lost a bet by walking spent the next fifty years getting paid to walk.
