# Facts: Samuel Franklin Cody (born Cowdery, Iowa; American Wild West showman in England, unrelated to Buffalo Bill) patented his winged box kite in 1901 and flew man-lifting kites. The Admiralty tested them at Woolwich (March 1903) and Portsmouth, on Whale Island and on ships (29 March to 18 April 1903), and ordered four sets. For publicity he planned a Channel crossing in a Berthon collapsible boat named Lela, towed by kites; for a first attempt from Dover to Calais with a Daily Mail reporter he tied life-lines with wooden balls to the boat ("if we are capsized we can catch these"), and the reporter went into town to buy a life-belt. On 5 November 1903 the wind at Dover was wrong, so he took the boat and kite on the 11 am mail boat to Calais. He left Calais at 7.30 pm towed by his two-year-old kite "Old Faithful". The wind dropped and the tide carried him toward the Goodwin Sands; the kite was lashed to the masthead, then put up again when the wind freshened. He landed, cold and tired, on the beach at Dover at 8.30 am on 6 November 1903 (about 13 hours). Dover Museum lists it as the first Channel crossing by kite, 1903. On 16 October 1908 Cody made the first officially recognised aeroplane flight in Britain.

In nineteen oh three, a man crossed the English Channel in a boat with no sail, no oars and no engine, pulled by a kite.
He was Samuel Franklin Cody, an American cowboy showman with a wide hat, a wider moustache, and kites that could lift a man.
The navy had tested them that spring, and now Cody wanted fame.
First he planned to leave Dover with a reporter, and tied lifelines along the boat in case it flipped.
The reporter took one look, and went into town to buy a lifebelt.
Then the wind turned the wrong way.
So on the fifth of November, he took his boat and kite to Calais on the mail steamer.
At half past seven that evening he pushed off, and his old kite climbed into the dark.
Out in the Channel the wind died, and the tide dragged him toward the Goodwin Sands.
You see, a kite is a sail on a very long string, and up high the wind runs faster, like a river in the middle, far from its banks.
When the breeze returned, the kite towed him on.
At half past eight the next morning, about thirteen hours later, Cody landed on the beach at Dover.
Five years later he made the first official aeroplane flight in Britain.
The kite man had finally let go of the string.
