# Facts: Benjamin Franklin, living in London in the early 1770s, described in a letter to William Brownrigg dated 7 November 1773 (printed in the Philosophical Transactions in 1774) how, in 1757 at sea in a fleet, he saw the wakes of two ships remarkably smooth and the captain said the cooks had been emptying their greasy water through the scuppers; later, on a windy day at a pond on Clapham Common, he fetched out a cruet of oil and dropped a little on the leeward side, where the wind drove it back to the shore; on the windward side "not more than a tea spoonful" made an instant calm over several yards square that spread until perhaps half an acre was "as smooth as a looking-glass"; afterwards he carried oil in the hollow upper joint of his bamboo cane; in October 1773 a sea trial at Portsmouth did not stop the surf but left a tract of smoothed water. In 1890 Lord Rayleigh spread a tiny measured amount of olive oil on water in a tray and calculated the film's thickness as about 1.6 x 10^-7 cm, about the length of one oil molecule; Franklin's own volume and area give a similar figure, but he never did the calculation.

In the seventeen seventies Benjamin Franklin calmed the waves on a windy pond with one teaspoon of oil.
He lived in London, famous for his lightning tests, and he could never leave a puzzle alone.
Years before at sea he saw the water behind two ships lying flat, and the captain said the cooks had just tipped out greasy water.
So on a gusty day at Clapham Common he took a little cruet of oil to a rippling pond.
He poured it on the downwind side and the wind blew it straight back to the bank.
So he went round to the windward side and tipped in barely a teaspoon.
The ripples died and the calm spread until half an acre lay smooth as glass.
The oil had vanished, yet the pond stayed flat.
You see, wind makes waves the way your fingers wrinkle a tablecloth by dragging it, and oil makes the water too slick to grip.
And oil spreads like butter on toast, thinner and thinner, until it is one molecule thick.
Franklin then hid oil in the top of his bamboo cane, ready for any pond.
A century later Lord Rayleigh measured that film in a tray, and it was the length of one molecule.
Franklin's numbers held the same answer, but he never did the sum.
