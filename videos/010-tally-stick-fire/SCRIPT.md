# Facts: 16 October 1834, the old Palace of Westminster, London. Since medieval times the Exchequer had kept its accounts on tally sticks: notched pieces of wood, normally willow, split in two so each side of an agreement held a matching half. Paper records had replaced them; in October 1834 the Clerk of Works, Richard Weobley, was told to clear the old sticks (about two large cartloads) while Parliament was adjourned. He rejected giving them to parliamentary staff as firewood and had them burned in the two furnaces under the House of Lords, which were designed for coal, not wood. Two Irish labourers, Joshua Cross and Patrick Furlong, began at dawn. At about 4 pm two visitors could not see the Armada tapestries in the Lords for smoke and felt heat through their boots near Black Rod's box. Shortly after 4 pm the workmen put in the last sticks, closed the furnace doors and left for the Star and Garter pub. Shortly after 5 pm heat and sparks from a flue ignited the woodwork above. At 6 pm a doorkeeper's wife saw flames under the Lords' door / Black Rod's box alight. At 6:30 pm a flashover produced a fireball visible from Windsor Castle, about 20 miles away. Crowds blocked Westminster Bridge and watched from boats on the Thames. Westminster Hall was saved (James Braidwood soaked its roof; the wind changed); the Lords, Commons, Painted Chamber and Speaker's House were destroyed. No one died. A Privy Council committee found the burning of the tallies caused the fire. Prime Minister Melbourne called it "one of the greatest instances of stupidity upon record."

In eighteen thirty-four, two workmen burned down the Houses of Parliament by throwing out old receipts.
For centuries the treasury kept its accounts on tally sticks, which were notched willow sticks split in two.
Each side kept one half, and the notches had to match, but now the accounts were on paper and two cartloads of sticks were in the way.
The clerk of works had them burned in the furnaces under the House of Lords.
On the sixteenth of October, Joshua Cross and Patrick Furlong fed them in from dawn, and by the afternoon visitors upstairs felt the floor hot through their boots.
Shortly after four the workmen shut the furnace doors and went to the pub, and just after six a doorkeeper's wife saw flames under the door of the Lords.
You see, those furnaces were built for coal, which glows low, but dry wood burns fast and tall.
It is like stuffing a toaster with newspaper, flames shoot out the top.
Hot gas raced up the flues and the wooden panelling above caught, and at half past six a fireball burst out as crowds packed the bridge to watch, and by morning only the great medieval hall was left.
Centuries of careful receipts, and the final bill was the Houses of Parliament.
