# Facts: Autumn 1872. An equine influenza first appeared in late September among horses pastured outside Toronto and became a North American epidemic (the "Canadian horse disease"), reaching New York on 21 October 1872, where the first signs (a light cough) appeared in a streetcar stable; whole stables could be immobilised within a day, the horses feverish and refusing food. New York had about one million people and tens of thousands of working horses (estimates range from 38,000 to 70,000). Oxen were tried but were "incapable of working city streets"; people ended up pulling streetcars, stages, drays, fire engines and carts (a photo shows conductors and passengers pulling a streetcar). Freight piled up at depots and piers. Only two of New York's fire horses (of about 144-150) escaped the flu; in Boston only three of eighty fire horses escaped it. On 9 November 1872 a fire broke out in downtown Boston; the first engine to arrive was pulled by men, not horses, and it took a long time to reach the scene; about sixty-five acres (64-65) burned before the fire was out on 11 November.

In eighteen seventy-two, New Yorkers had to pull their own streetcars, because nearly every horse in town had the flu.
The city was proud of its busy streets, where horses pulled every streetcar, cart and fire engine.
That fall a strange cough spread south from Toronto, and in late October a New York stable hand heard his horses cough.
In a day whole stables stood still, the horses too sick to eat.
The streetcar firms tried oxen, but the slow beasts could not cope with the crowds.
So crews and riders grabbed ropes and pulled the cars along by hand.
Of all the fire horses in the city, just two stayed well, and on November ninth a fire broke out in Boston.
You see, a horse flu spreads the way a cold spreads through a school.
Put a lot of noses in one warm room with one water bucket, and by Friday one sniffle is everyone's sniffle.
In the city, stables packed a lot of horses side by side, so the flu went through them all in days.
In Boston the first fire engine had to be dragged to the blaze by men on foot.
By the time it was out, about sixty-five acres of the city were gone.
For a few weeks, the fastest thing in the city was a man with a rope.
