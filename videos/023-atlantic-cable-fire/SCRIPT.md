# Facts: The first transatlantic telegraph cable, 1858. Cyrus W. Field co-founded the Atlantic Telegraph Company in 1856; attempts in 1857 and June 1858 failed when the cable snapped. On 29 July 1858 HMS Agamemnon and USS Niagara spliced the cable in mid-Atlantic and steamed in opposite directions; Niagara reached Trinity Bay, Newfoundland, and Agamemnon Valentia, Ireland, on 5 August, linking about 3,200 km (about two thousand miles). On 16 August Queen Victoria's 98-word greeting to President James Buchanan took almost 16 hours to send. New York celebrated with illuminations and fireworks over City Hall Park; on the night of 17 August sparks got under the roof's metal covering and the cupola (dome) of City Hall burned, destroying the attic, roof, cupola and its Justice statue (Harper's Weekly cover, 28 August 1858). The signal was weak and degraded quickly; the electrician Wildman Whitehouse used up to 2,000 volts to boost it, and the inquiry blamed his high voltages (later historians add poor manufacture and insulation). 732 messages were sent; the line failed within a few weeks (declared a failure on 20 October 1858), and some writers questioned whether there had been a cable at all. A lasting cable was completed in 1866.

In eighteen fifty-eight, New York cheered a wire so hard that it set its own City Hall on fire.
The wire ran along the floor of the sea for about two thousand miles, from Ireland to Newfoundland.
Cyrus Field had seen cable after cable snap and sink, so that summer two warships met out in the middle, tied the ends, and steamed apart.
This time the line held.
Queen Victoria sent ninety-eight words to the President, and they took almost sixteen hours.
Nobody minded, and fireworks burst over City Hall.
Sparks got in under the roof, and by morning the dome was in flames.
But down in the sea the signal was fading, each click weaker than the last.
So the man in charge of the line, Wildman Whitehouse, just pushed harder, with up to two thousand volts.
You see, a worn cable is like an old garden hose with a crack in it.
Turn the tap all the way up and the water does not go further, it just bursts out of the crack.
Within weeks the line was dead, and some papers wondered if there had ever been a cable at all.
Eight years later Field laid one that worked.
But New York had burned its City Hall for a chat that lasted a few weeks.
