# Facts: London and Paris, July to September 1913. Max Mayer, a pearl dealer of Hatton Garden, owned a necklace of sixty-one blush-pink pearls (called the "Mona Lisa of pearls"), insured with Lloyd's (about 135,000 pounds per Wikipedia). He sent it to his agent in Paris (Henri Salomons) for a sale that fell through, and it was returned by registered post, wrapped, tied with string and sealed with wax seals. On 16 July 1913 Mayer opened the parcel: the seals looked intact, but the pearls had been replaced by lumps of sugar (eleven per Lloyd's), of the same weight per Lloyd's. Lloyd's underwriters worked with Scotland Yard (Chief Inspector Alfred Ward); the assessors offered a 10,000 pound reward. The gang of Joseph "Cammi" Grizzard, a jewel thief and fence known as the "King of Fences", with James Lockett, Simon Silverman and Leiser Gutwirth, had the parcel intercepted (per Wikipedia by bribing a postman). Informers and detectives followed the gang for weeks; police lured them to a meeting at Chancery Lane tube station and arrested them on 2 September 1913, but none had the pearls. About two weeks later the piano maker Augustus Horne, in Highbury, saw a man drop something in the gutter and run; inside a matchbox was a broken string of pearls. Lloyd's says he gave one pearl to a street child as a marble and handed the rest to the police, who identified them as Mayer's necklace. Grizzard and Lockett got seven years (Wikipedia).
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pearl_Robbery, https://www.lloyds.com/about-lloyds/history/catastrophes-and-claims/great-pearl-robbery
