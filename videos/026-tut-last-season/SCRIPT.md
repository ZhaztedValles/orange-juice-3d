# Facts: Valley of the Kings, Egypt. The American Theodore Davis gave up his concession in the valley in 1914, sure nothing more remained to be found (National Geographic); Lord Carnarvon and his archaeologist Howard Carter took it over and dug there from 1917. In 1922 a frustrated Carnarvon told Carter he would stop paying for the search; Carter pleaded, and Carnarvon agreed to fund one last season (National Geographic). Digging resumed on 1 November 1922 near the tomb of Ramesses VI. On 4 November 1922, while clearing ancient workmen's huts, Carter's team found the top step of a staircase cut into the bedrock; on 5 November a sealed, plastered doorway appeared. Carter covered it again and cabled Carnarvon: "At last have made wonderful discovery in Valley, a magnificent tomb with seals intact" (Griffith Institute). Carnarvon arrived in Luxor on 23 November, Lady Evelyn soon after. On 26 November Carter made a small hole in the doorway and looked in by candlelight; Carnarvon asked "Can you see anything?", Carter replied "Yes, wonderful things" (National Geographic), gold and ebony furniture, chariots, statues and caskets (Griffith Institute). The narration's "what if" part (Carnarvon saying no and what followed) is imagined.
Sources: https://tutankhamun.griffith.ox.ac.uk/seasons/1st-season-1922-1923, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/history-magazine/article/findingkingtutstomb

Egypt's most famous treasure was found because a rich man gave his digger one last chance.
For five years, Lord Carnarvon paid Howard Carter to dig in the Valley of the Kings for a lost boy king, Tutankhamun, in a spot where an American had given up, sure it was empty.
Carter moved tons of sand and found almost nothing, so the lord said the money was gone, but Carter begged, and he paid for one more winter.
In November nineteen twenty-two, under the ruins of old huts, Carter's men hit a step cut in the rock, and it led down to a sealed door, so the lord rushed out to see.
Carter held a candle to a small hole, the lord asked if he could see anything, and Carter said yes, wonderful things.
But what if the lord had said no that summer?
The huts would have stayed, and tourists would walk over that hidden step for years.
No gold mask in the papers, no songs about King Tut, no mummy's curse.
Maybe a new team would find it years later, or thieves first.
But in real life the lord said yes one more time, and the most famous tomb of all was waiting under huts no one had bothered to move.
