# Facts: In late 1870 the Kentucky cousins Philip Arnold and John Slack showed the San Francisco financier George D. Roberts a bag of rough diamonds and would not say where they came from. William C. Ralston of the Bank of California, Asbury Harpending, William Lent and others joined in. In October 1871 Charles Lewis Tiffany valued a sample (about a tenth of the stones) at about $150,000 in New York. In July 1871 the cousins had bought about $20,000 of cheap rough diamonds and rubies in London under false names. On 4 June 1872 the mining engineer Henry Janin, led to the site near the Colorado-Wyoming line (blindfolded for the final leg), found stones quickly and declared the field genuine; only a little over one acre was salted. Arnold took about $550,000 and Slack about $100,000. In October 1872 the geologist Clarence King of the 40th Parallel Survey, tipped off by Samuel Emmons, examined the site: gems lay only in disturbed ground, and anthills had small holes with a gem at the bottom of each. King called it an "unparalleled fraud" and the San Francisco and New York Mining and Commercial Company was dissolved; the exposure was published on 26 November 1872. USGS maps still label the place "Diamond Field".

In eighteen seventy-two, the richest bankers in San Francisco bought a diamond field that two men had sprinkled by hand.
Two cousins, Philip Arnold and John Slack, showed a banker a bag of rough diamonds, but they would not say where they found them.
Soon the big banks wanted in, and Tiffany put a sample at a hundred and fifty thousand dollars.
Their expert, Henry Janin, was led out to a far off mesa with his eyes covered for the last part.
In no time they were picking diamonds and rubies out of the dirt, Janin said it was real, and the cousins got over half a million dollars.
Then a geologist, Clarence King, rode out to see it for himself.
The gems were only where the ground had been dug, and on the anthills, each ruby sat at the end of a tiny hole.
You see, diamonds and rubies grow in very different rocks, so you almost never find them in the same spot.
It is like finding an apple, a banana and a coconut under one small bush, someone put them there.
The cousins had bought cheap stones in London for about twenty thousand dollars and thrown them over an acre.
King called it a fraud, and the company was shut down.
The maps still call it Diamond Field, and there is not one diamond in it.
