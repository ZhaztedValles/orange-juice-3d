# Facts: Munich, Bavaria, 1798 (published in the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society that year). Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford (born in Massachusetts in 1753, made a count of the Holy Roman Empire in 1791), superintended the boring of cannon in the workshops of the military arsenal at Munich. Brass guns were cast solid and then bored; he noticed the very considerable heat a brass gun acquires in a short time while being bored, and the still hotter metal chips. For his experiment he took an unbored brass six-pounder casting, turned its end into a solid cylinder about seven and three quarter inches across and nearly ten inches long, and pressed a blunt steel borer against it with a force of about ten thousand pounds while the casting turned about thirty-two times a minute, driven by horses (two were used; he says one would have been enough). The cylinder sat in a watertight box (made of deal, a soft wood) holding about eighteen and three quarter pounds (about two and a quarter gallons) of water at sixty degrees Fahrenheit. After one hour the water was at one hundred and seven degrees, after an hour and a half one hundred and forty-two, after two hours one hundred and seventy-eight, after two hours twenty minutes two hundred, and after two hours thirty minutes it actually boiled. He wrote that it would be difficult to describe the surprise and astonishment on the faces of the bystanders at seeing so much cold water heated and made to boil without any fire. He concluded that the heat seemed inexhaustible and could not be a material substance (the caloric fluid of the theory of his day), but must be motion.

In seventeen ninety-eight, a man in Munich boiled a box of water without lighting a fire.
He was Count Rumford, who ran the army workshops of Bavaria, and he was proud of his cannon.
Each brass gun was cast solid, and horses turned it against a steel drill to bore out the barrel.
Rumford saw the metal get so hot that nobody could touch it.
So he sank a cannon casting in a wooden box of cold water and fitted a blunt drill.
Two horses walked in circles, the tool ground on the brass, and in an hour the water was warm.
After two hours, nobody could keep a hand in it.
Then, at two and a half hours, with the workmen crowding round, the water boiled.
There was no coal, no flame, and the bystanders stared in astonishment.
You see, heat is not a thing hiding inside metal, it is motion.
Rub your hands together on a cold morning and they warm up, though nothing was poured into them.
The grinding shook the tiny bits of brass faster and faster, and that shaking is what we feel as heat.
Scientists then said heat was an invisible fluid, but Rumford's horses just kept making more.
In the end, the best stove in Munich was two horses walking in a circle.
