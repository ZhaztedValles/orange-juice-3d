# Facts: Royal Polytechnic Institution, Regent Street, London; first public show on 24 December 1862 (Christmas Eve), a ghost scene from an adaptation of Charles Dickens's The Haunted Man (Scientific American; Wikipedia; one account names another play, so the narration does not name it). Henry Dircks, a Liverpool civil engineer, had worked out the illusion and from 1858 tried to sell it to theatres, but his design needed a costly rebuilding of the auditorium and owners were not interested. In late 1862 John Henry Pepper, chemist and lecturer at the Polytechnic, saw one of Dircks's models and adapted it: the "ghost" actor was hidden in the orchestra pit (a room under/in front of the stage), lit strongly by an arc lamp, and a large sheet of plate glass, tilted slightly toward the audience, stood between the audience and the stage. Plain glass acts as a window or a mirror depending on the light on each side: stage bright and pit dark, the ghost is invisible; reverse the lighting and the reflection appears. Actors could not see the ghost, so Pepper hid marks on the floor for their feet. The 500-seat theatre was full for 15 months; the Prince of Wales brought his bride to see it. By late 1863 ghost plays ran in London, Manchester, Glasgow, Paris and New York, and demand for glass screens caused a shortage of plate glass. Pepper and Dircks took out a joint patent (provisional February 1863); Dircks gave up any share of the profits; Pepper used his own name with the public, and the effect is still called Pepper's ghost.

On Christmas Eve eighteen sixty-two, a ghost walked onto a London stage, and nobody could tell how.
The stage was the Royal Polytechnic, and its star was John Henry Pepper, a chemist who loved a crowd.
But the idea was not his.
A Liverpool engineer, Henry Dircks, had dreamed it up years before, but his plan meant rebuilding the whole theatre.
Pepper found a cheaper way.
He hid an actor in the pit below the stage, and lit him with a blazing lamp.
Then he stood a huge sheet of glass in front of the stage, tilted toward the seats.
The stage lights dimmed, and a pale figure drifted beside the actors.
They could not see it, so they followed secret marks on the floor.
Five hundred people stared at a ghost that was not there.
You see, glass is a window and a mirror at once, like your kitchen window at night, when you see yourself floating in the dark garden.
Light the hidden actor, dim the stage, and the glass shows both.
The show sold out for fifteen months, and soon London ran short of plate glass.
Dircks gave up the money, and the crowds only heard one name.
It is still called Pepper's ghost.
The man who dreamed it up became the one nobody could see.
