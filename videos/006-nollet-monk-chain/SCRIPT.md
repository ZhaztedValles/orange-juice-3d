# Facts: In 1746 Jean-Antoine Nollet, a French deacon known as the Abbé Nollet who popularised physics in Paris with public lectures and instruments, discharged a battery of Leyden jars (the jar that stores an electric charge, invented in 1745) through a human chain: about two hundred Carthusian monks in a circle about a mile (about 1.6 km) around, linked by pieces of iron wire (some accounts say rods). Each man reacted at substantially the same time to the shock, showing that electricity travels very fast; Nollet concluded it spread almost instantly. Louis XV had the demonstration done at Versailles with a company of 180 royal guards holding hands, who leapt simultaneously (sources differ on which came first). Some accounts give other numbers (up to 700 monks, a 5,400-foot circle); the narration uses "about two hundred" and "about a mile". Today electrical signals in wires are known to travel at a large fraction of the speed of light.

In seventeen forty-six a French priest made two hundred monks leap into the air at exactly the same moment.
Jean-Antoine Nollet taught science in Paris, and he adored his new toy, a glass jar that could store a spark.
Touch the jar and the shock jolted your arms like a kick.
Nollet wanted to know how fast electricity travels.
So he lined up about two hundred monks in a giant ring, about a mile around.
Each monk held an iron wire that joined him to the next.
Nollet charged his jar and touched it to the first wire.
If the shock crawled like a messenger, a wave of jumping monks would race around the ring.
Instead every single monk yelped and jumped at the very same instant.
Not one monk was a blink behind.
You see, a wire works like a garden hose that is already full of water.
Open the tap and water spills from the far end at once, because the whole hose pushes together.
For the king at Versailles, he made a hundred and eighty royal guards leap together too.
So Nollet decided that electricity must be instant.
It is not, it moves at nearly the speed of light, and across a mile of monks that is the same thing.
