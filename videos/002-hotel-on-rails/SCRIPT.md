# Facts: Brighton Beach, Coney Island, Brooklyn, spring 1888; the shoreline was eroding and for about two years the ocean had been washing under the hotel itself, part of which stood on piles; the owner also ran the Brooklyn and Brighton Beach Railroad; workers jacked the three-story hotel onto flatcars (about 112 to 124, sources differ) running on about two dozen parallel tracks; six steam locomotives pulled it with hawsers spread across its roughly 400-foot width; it weighed about 4,000 tons (about eight million pounds); it moved 117 feet on the first day and the rest of the roughly 600 feet (sources say 500 to 600) the following week; it stood on its new dry site until it was demolished in the 1920s (1923 to 1926 in different sources).
Sources: https://americanheritage.com/1888-one-hundred-years-ago-1, https://www.bklynlibrary.org/blog/2019/04/10/brighton-beach-hotel-1888

In eighteen eighty-eight, six steam locomotives dragged an entire seaside hotel away from the ocean.
It's Brighton Beach, on Coney Island, and the grand hotel is the proudest building there, but every storm bites off more beach, and for two years the waves have sloshed right under the porch.
The owner could build a wall, or move the hotel, and he happens to own a railroad.
So workers jack the building up, slide flatcars underneath, and lay two dozen tracks side by side, and six locomotives hook on with thick ropes while a crowd gathers to watch a hotel go for a walk.
The wheels turn, and the building creeps one hundred seventeen feet on day one.
It weighs four thousand tons, enough to crush rails and snap ropes, yet nothing snaps.
You see, a heavy load is like a person walking in deep snow, since boots sink, but snowshoes spread the weight out, so you stay on top.
Two dozen tracks and over a hundred flatcars are snowshoes for a hotel.
Within a couple of weeks it has rolled about six hundred feet inland, onto dry ground, and there it stands for over thirty years, safe from the surf.
The sea came for the hotel, and the hotel just took the train.
