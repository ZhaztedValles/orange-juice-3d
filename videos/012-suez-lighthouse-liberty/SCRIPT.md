# Facts: The French sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi (1834-1904) travelled in Egypt in the 1850s and was struck by the colossal statues of Abu Simbel. In the late 1860s, while the Suez Canal was being built, he designed "Egypt (or Progress) Carrying the Light to Asia": a colossal robed Egyptian woman (a fellah) holding a torch, a lighthouse for the canal's entrance at Port Said, about 26 m (86 ft) tall on a 15 m (48 ft) pedestal. He showed it to Khedive Isma'il Pasha, the ruler funding the canal; the Khedive declined, citing the cost. The canal opened on 17 November 1869, and the Port Said Lighthouse by François Coignet was built at the same place in 1869. Bartholdi reworked the design into "Liberty Enlightening the World", the Statue of Liberty, dedicated in New York Harbor in 1886; he later insisted the two projects were unrelated, a claim historians doubt given his sketches and early models. The narration's "what if" part (the Khedive saying yes and what followed) is imagined.

The Statue of Liberty was almost a lighthouse in Egypt.
In the eighteen fifties, a young French sculptor, Auguste Bartholdi, stood under the huge stone kings of Abu Simbel and came home dreaming of giants.
Years later, as the Suez Canal was being dug, he drew his own: a robed Egyptian woman as tall as a church, at the mouth of the canal.
Her torch would guide the ships in from the sea all night.
He took his drawings to Egypt's ruler, the Khedive, and the Khedive said no, she cost too much.
So when the canal opened in eighteen sixty-nine, Port Said got a plain lighthouse.
But what if the Khedive had said yes?
Every ship from Europe would sail in under a giant woman with her lamp blazing over the water.
Sailors would take her picture to every port in the world.
And Bartholdi, with his giant already standing, might never dream up a second one.
Because in real life, he kept the idea.
He later said the two had nothing to do with each other, but his drawings look like sisters.
In eighteen eighty-six, a robed woman with a torch was lit in New York Harbor.
So the most famous welcome in America was first drawn for Egypt.
