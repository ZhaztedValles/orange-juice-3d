# Chapter 3: The Guns of Toulon (facts and sources: ../SCRIPT.md)

<!-- narration -->
The French Revolution began in the summer of seventeen eighty-nine.
Within four years, the king was gone, executed in Paris in January seventeen ninety-three, and France was at war with half of Europe.
The Revolution needed officers, and many of the old noble officers had fled the country.
Suddenly, a talented young man without connections could climb very fast.
That summer, the great naval port of Toulon, in the south of France, rebelled against the government in Paris.
It opened its harbour to a British and Spanish fleet.
For the young Republic this was a disaster, because Toulon held a large part of the French navy.
A French army surrounded the town, but the siege was going nowhere.
Then the officer in charge of the army's cannons was wounded, and a twenty-four-year-old captain was sent to replace him.
It was Napoleon.
He studied the maps and saw what the others had missed.
You did not need to attack the town itself.
On a hill above the harbour, the British had built a fort so strong that it was nicknamed Little Gibraltar.
Whoever held that hill could fire on every ship in the harbour below.
Take the fort, and the British fleet would have to leave.
So Napoleon gathered every cannon he could find.
He set up his batteries close to the enemy walls, so close that his gunners were under constant fire.
He named one of them the battery of the men without fear.
On the night of the seventeenth of December, in pouring rain, the French stormed the fort.
Napoleon joined the attack, and was wounded in the thigh by a British bayonet.
But the fort fell.
Within two days, the British fleet sailed away, and Toulon was back in French hands.
The reward came fast.
A few days later, at just twenty-four, Napoleon was promoted to brigadier general.
But in revolutionary France, success could be very dangerous.
